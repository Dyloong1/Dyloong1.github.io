Teaching Philosophy Statement

  1. I believe students learn best when they are taught the latest knowledge, moving from the high-level picture down to the details, and when they are given a playground, or multiple approaches beyond words alone, to understand and practice what they learn. For example, when teaching a difficult concept, I start with visualizations and demos.
  2. As an educator, my role is to teach my students to find their own first principles in every domain and to always look for the high-level guidance behind everything. I want my students to learn knowledge, and more than just knowledge: how to acquire it and how to question it with a clear mind. I also want to support my students’ mental health.
  3. One way I connect course content to students’ prior knowledge or experience is by extending from their existing understanding and finding the underlying similarities and differences. I always start from the high level, with clear motivation and a clear chain of thought.
  4. I know that meaningful learning has happened when students keep hold of the high-level ideas and motivations, and are able to respond, ask questions, and form their own understanding.
  5. I will continue to develop my teaching by, first, improving how I understand the world and knowledge; second, keeping up with state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods and research; and finally, teaching more and more students. Because students are diverse, I will learn how to generalize my teaching so that it benefits as many students as possible.

Learning Goals, Objectives & Outcomes

Course: Algorithms

Goal

Solve real-life problems using algorithmic thinking: starting from first principles, students learn to turn problems described in natural language into well-defined computational problems and to design efficient algorithms that solve them.

Objectives

  1. Introduce greedy algorithms through intuitive real-life examples, including cases where the greedy choice fails, so that students learn to question their first intuition.
  2. Guide students from greedy algorithms to dynamic programming using the most representative DP problems, building the mindset of identifying repeated subproblems.

Outcomes

  1. Given a real-life problem described in natural language, students will be able to formulate it as a computational problem (inputs, outputs, and constraints) and justify whether a greedy strategy gives an optimal solution, providing a counterexample when it does not.
  2. Students will be able to identify the repeated subproblems in a problem, define a DP recurrence, implement the solution, and analyze its time and space complexity.

Course Design

Course: Algorithms

  1. Start from first principles: real-life problems described in natural language.
  2. Begin with the most straightforward but tricky algorithms, such as greedy algorithms, to spark students’ interest.
  3. Go deeper into dynamic programming (DP), which can also be a very engaging topic. Identifying repeated subproblems is the most interesting, important, and difficult mindset for CS students to develop.
  4. Start with the most representative DP problems.

Syllabus Review

Reviewed: CIS 108 Programming Fundamentals, Standard Syllabus 2024–25 (Passaic County Community College)

What does the syllabus value?

It values content coverage and rules. Most of the document is a chapter-by-chapter list of Python topics and a set of policies. It says very little about why students should learn programming, what motivates each topic, or who the instructor is and how to reach them.

How are students expected to spend their time?

Students spend 3 lecture hours per week in class and are expected to complete about 6 more hours of work outside class each week, so roughly 9 hours per week. All projects are done outside class, and there are no lab hours, even though lab projects are 30% of the grade.

What behavior is rewarded?

Tests, quizzes, and the final exam together count for 65% of the grade, lab projects for 30%, and attendance and activities for only 5%. This mainly rewards individual performance on timed exams, rather than building programs, explaining ideas, or collaborating.

Are the goals aligned with the assessments?

Only partially. The early outcomes (algorithms, selection, loops, strings, and lists) match the weekly schedule. However, outcomes 5–8 (classes and objects, documentation, graphics and image processing, and GUIs) never appear in the schedule, which ends with lists and text files. The schedule also says a final project is due in week 15, but there is no final project in the grading breakdown.

What might make students anxious?

Missed exams cannot be made up after the exam date, and make-ups require prior notification and original documentation. Students must earn a C or better to move on to CIS 160 and CIS 165, but no grade scale is given. There are no office hours, instructor contact information, or late-work policy, and the schedule is “subject to change.” A beginner reading this would know the penalties but not where to get help.

What are the key sections, and what is missing?

The key sections are the course learning outcomes, the grading breakdown, the weekly schedule, the make-up exam policy, and the accessibility accommodations. If I revised this syllabus, I would add a short motivation for the course, instructor contact information and office hours, a grade scale, a late-work policy, a policy on AI tools like ChatGPT, and support resources such as tutoring and the open lab. I would also update the 2013 textbook and align the schedule and the assessments with every stated outcome.
